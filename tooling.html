§ 01 Origin

The consolidation was the decision

Call attribution lived in a Google Form feeding a spreadsheet. Invoices were made in separate software. Tire counts were tracked by hand. Quotes were written by hand with no link to the job they became. Expenses and customer accounts weren't tracked at all.

A call becomes a job. The job consumes tires, produces an invoice, generates expenses, and belongs to a customer. One event, recorded five times, in five places that never agreed.

The tool started as tire inventory and grew into everything above as the real workflow became clear. The name stuck from the first version, which is honest about how it happened — the scope was discovered by using it, not planned up front.

§ 02 Method

How this was built

This was built with AI assistance, in Claude Code. My work was the product side: identifying that five systems were one event, specifying the domain rules from how the business actually runs, deciding what belonged in the tool and what didn't, and operating it in production while the owner used it daily.

The architecture and implementation were AI-generated under that direction. I'm stating that plainly because it's how the work happened, and because shipping a system a business depends on — and keeping it running — is the part that transfers.

What I'd want to be asked about

The domain modeling and the operational decisions: why a quote number is assigned to every job but an invoice number only at completion, why voiding a completed job restocks inventory and voiding a quote doesn't, and what it's like to run schema changes against a database a business is actively using. Those I can speak to. The deeper implementation internals I'd be learning alongside you.

§ 03 System

What it does

  • Jobs — one record from first call through quote, completion, and paid invoice.
  • Quotes and invoices — generated as documents from the job record, with per-line price overrides.
  • Tire inventory — counts and average cost, decremented when a job completes.
  • Expenses — categorized against a Schedule C list for tax time.
  • Fleet accounts — repeat commercial customers with payment terms.
  • Job photos — damage and before/after proof, stored in R2.

Cloudflare Workers serving both the API and the frontend, D1 for relational data, R2 for images. Three runtime dependencies, no build step. Passkey authentication with invite-gated registration — there are two legitimate users and there always will be.

§ 04 Operating

Running it while it's in use

The app went into daily use early and stayed there. Every schema change after the first week had to apply to a database with real rows in it, while the owner was mid-week and taking calls. There's no staging environment, so a bug isn't a failing test — it's an invoice with the wrong number, sent to a customer.

Practically that meant: 24 migrations written to be additive rather than destructive, a full database export before every production change, and an automated test suite that has to pass before anything merges. Merging deploys automatically, so that test run is the last gate before the business is using the change.

Rows are never hard-deleted. A mis-entered purchase is reversed and marked rather than erased, so it stays available for audit but drops out of reporting totals. That's a bookkeeping requirement, not a technical one.
